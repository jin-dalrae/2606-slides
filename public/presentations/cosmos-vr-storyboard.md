# COSMOS VR STORYBOARD

## Jeenie — browsing when hands, posture, and attention are fragmented

<p class="deck-subtitle">User storyboard: why a spatial wall beats a desk browser in postpartum life</p>

Presenter: Rae Jin  
July 30, 2026

Note: This deck is a design storyboard, not a research report. Jeenie is a composite persona. Use it to pressure-test Cosmos interaction assumptions (gaze, hand-pull, world-anchored wall, passthrough).

---

## The spine of this story

### Cosmos is for people who cannot sit at a desk and hold a phone

Jeenie is two weeks postpartum. Her days are feed → change → nap → chores → rest, with almost no clean blocks of attention.

She still wants to stay current — the techie habit of scanning news and gossip fast — but her body and her baby will not cooperate with desk-browser VR or long phone sessions.

<p class="slide-closing">If Cosmos only works for seated, two-handed, full-attention browsing, it fails the people who need spatial async most.</p>

## Her day has no clean "me time"

<p class="slide-ref">01 · Time split</p>

When the baby sleeps, she does not get a free hour. She gets a fragile pocket.

In that pocket she tries to stack chores, rest, and a little of her old self — catch up on the world before the next wake.

She feels awkward about how to split the time: productive parent, recovering body, or the person who used to know what was happening online.

<p class="slide-closing">Browsing has to fit inside interrupted pockets, not replace them with a session.</p>

---

## VR looks like a way out — then the browser fails her

<p class="slide-ref">03–04 · VR hope vs desk browser</p>

<div class="cards-2">
  <article>
    <h3>Why she tries a headset</h3>
    <p>She can lie down with the baby. Looking around should be easier than holding a glowing rectangle at arm's length.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h3>Why the usual browser fails</h3>
    <p>Web VR is still desk-shaped: lean in, read close, use hands. Looking at sites up close feels bad. Moving a hand sometimes hits the baby on her chest.</p>
  </article>
</div>

<p class="slide-closing">The headset is not the problem. The interaction model is still a monitor with extra steps.</p>

---

## She finds Cosmos: posts on a wall, arranged by similarity

<p class="slide-ref">05 · Discovery</p>

She searches for the best VR browsing app and finds **Cosmos** in the App Store.

Instead of a floating desktop browser, content lives on a **wall** — posts arranged by similarity, built for headset distance rather than desk-monitor reading.

<p class="slide-closing">The first product promise she understands is not "social VR." It is "I can scan the world without a feed and without a desk."</p>

---

## Gaze turns the head into a cursor; a hand only pulls when needed

<p class="slide-ref">06 · Interaction</p>

<div class="cards-2">
  <article>
    <h3>Eyes look around</h3>
    <p>Looking is enough to browse. She does not need to aim a controller for every shift of attention.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h3>Hand pulls the wall closer</h3>
    <p>When she wants detail, a simple hand pull brings the wall toward her — then she can look across posts without a tool in her grip.</p>
  </article>
</div>

<p class="slide-closing">Controller-free browse is not a nicety here — it is what keeps the baby from getting bumped.</p>

---

## When she stands, the wall stays in the room

<p class="slide-ref">07 · World-anchored browse</p>

She gets up and walks. The wall does not follow her face like a HUD. It stays put.

She stops pulling. She walks along the wall the way people walk a bulletin board — scan, step closer, step back, change angle.

Because the **background is transparent (passthrough)**, she can still see the house and avoid furniture, pets, and clutter.

<p class="slide-closing">Place memory only works if the wall is a place — not a sticker glued to the camera.</p>

---

## Care does not pause the session

<p class="slide-ref">08 · Continuous use</p>

The baby wakes hungry. Jeenie breastfeeds and **keeps the headset on**.

She does not need a clean "VR session" that ends when parenting starts. The wall can wait in the room while her hands and chest are occupied; gaze and light head movement are still available.

<p class="slide-closing">Success is not longer sessions. Success is sessions that survive real interruptions.</p>

## What Jeenie's story demands from Cosmos

<p class="slide-ref">Design implications</p>

| Constraint from the story | Product requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Wrist pain / short phone tolerance | Minimize sustained grip; favor gaze and occasional hand gestures |
| Baby on chest / sling | Avoid frequent mid-air hand waving near the torso; controller optional for browse |
| Lying down + standing | Comfortable at recline; wall readable without leaning into a virtual monitor |
| Indoors, moving around home | World-anchored content + passthrough so the body stays safe in real space |
| Fragmented attention | Browsing must be scannable in pockets — similarity clusters beat endless scroll |
| Identity: still wants the world | High-signal wall of posts/news, not a social hangout that demands performance |

## Desk browser vs Cosmos wall (for this user)

<p class="slide-ref">Competitive contrast inside one body</p>

<div class="cards-2">
  <article>
    <h3>Desk-shaped VR browser</h3>
    <p>Assumes seated posture, close reading, and free hands. Collides with baby, wrist pain, and recline. Content has no place memory in the room.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h3>Cosmos wall</h3>
    <p>Assumes walk-or-recline browse, gaze-first attention, optional pull, and a fixed wall with passthrough. Content stays where she left it when life interrupts.</p>
  </article>
</div>

<p class="slide-closing">Jeenie is not asking for more immersion. She is asking for less friction between information and care.</p>

---

## What this storyboard does not prove

<p class="slide-ref">Honesty check</p>

This is a **scenario**, not evidence. It does not prove postpartum users will adopt headsets, or that gaze+pull is comfortable for 20 minutes, or that passthrough is safe enough around a newborn.

What it does is name the failure modes product tests must hit:

1. Can someone browse usefully while reclining with occupied hands?
2. Does a world-anchored wall beat a follow-head browser for place memory and safety?
3. Can a session survive a care interruption without a full restart?

<p class="slide-closing">Treat Jeenie as a stress test for interaction assumptions — then validate with real users who share her constraints.</p>

---

## Closing claim

### If Cosmos works for Jeenie, it works for anyone whose body is not "desk-ready"

The wall is not decoration. For users with fragmented hands, posture, and time, spatial async is the only browsing model that can stay in the room while life continues.

Build for gaze-first scan, optional hand pull, world-anchored placement, and passthrough safety — or admit the product is still a monitor for people with free afternoons.

<p class="slide-closing">Rebuild the wall for the body people actually have.</p>
